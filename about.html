About This Tool

This website helps Arizona better understand how artificial intelligence may affect occupations, workers, and career pathways across the state. It brings together occupational task information, labor market data, AI exposure research, employment trends, and pathway analysis to identify where AI-related disruption may be more likely and where lower-risk transition opportunities may exist.

The core measure used on this site is an AI Displacement Index. Unlike a theoretical exposure score alone, this index is designed to go beyond asking whether an occupation’s tasks could be affected by AI. It also incorporates labor market signals that help assess whether exposed occupations are showing signs of actual employment vulnerability, displacement pressure, or transition risk.

After selecting an occupation at higher AI displacement risk, users can distinguish between Direct Pathways and Indirect Pathways. Direct Pathways identify occupations that are most closely related to the source occupation based on similarities in skills, knowledge, work activities, and experience requirements. Indirect Pathways are also provided to help uncover additional career options that may not appear as direct matches, but are connected through related occupations. These pathways can reveal occupations that may offer strong long-term transition opportunities by drawing on transferable skills and experience from the source occupation.

The purpose of this website is not to predict that specific jobs will disappear. Rather, it provides a data-informed planning tool to help workers, workforce partners, educators, employers, and policymakers understand where AI may change job duties, skill requirements, hiring demand, or career mobility.

Why This Matters

Artificial intelligence is expected to reshape many jobs, but the effects will not be evenly distributed across occupations, regions, or groups of workers. National research consistently finds that AI exposure does not necessarily mean job loss. Instead, exposure identifies places in the labor market where AI may change how work is performed, which tasks are valued, and what skills workers need to remain competitive.

The Yale Budget Lab notes that occupational AI exposure is not the same as predicting which jobs will be automated out of existence; it identifies where AI could have an impact. This distinction matters for Arizona's workforce system because some occupations may experience task automation or declining demand, while others may see productivity gains, new skill requirements, or growth in related roles.

For workers, the key policy question is not simply, "Will AI replace my job?" A better question is: "How do I prepare for the AI transition?"

How Our Approach Is Different

Many AI risk tools focus primarily on theoretical exposure. These approaches typically estimate whether the tasks within an occupation can be performed, assisted, or transformed by AI. That is useful, but it does not always tell us whether workers in those occupations are actually facing displacement risk in the labor market.

The AI Displacement Index used on this website is different because it combines theoretical exposure with labor market evidence. Instead of relying only on whether AI could affect an occupation, the index also considers whether an occupation appears vulnerable based on employment patterns, displacement-related indicators, and Arizona-specific labor market context.

This distinction is important because some highly exposed occupations may remain stable or even grow if AI increases productivity or demand. Other occupations may have only moderate theoretical exposure but still show signs of labor market vulnerability due to weak growth, limited transferability, low wages, or poor transition options.

The index should therefore be understood as a workforce planning measure, not a prediction of automation. It is intended to help identify where Arizona may need targeted reskilling, upskilling, career navigation, employer engagement, or training investment.

Who Is Vulnerable to Job Displacement

Workers are most vulnerable to AI-related displacement when several risks overlap. High AI exposure alone does not necessarily mean a worker is at high risk. Vulnerability increases when an occupation is exposed to AI and also shows declining labor market demand, limited related career pathways, lower wage mobility, or skill requirements that do not transfer easily into growing occupations.

National research suggests that AI exposure is concentrated differently than earlier waves of automation. Earlier automation risk often focused on lower-wage or less-educated workers in routine manual or production roles. Generative AI exposure is more concentrated in office, clerical, administrative, analytical, and professional tasks.

Some highly exposed workers may have strong adaptive capacity because they have transferable skills, higher earnings, more education, or access to growing related occupations. For this reason, this tool considers workers who may face multiple barriers at once. These workers may benefit most from proactive workforce services, training alignment, career counseling, and employer partnerships.

What Users Can Explore

Users can use this website to explore how AI displacement risk varies across occupations in Arizona. The site allows users to compare occupations by risk level, employment size, wage, projected growth, annual openings, and typical education requirements.

Users can also explore related occupations that may offer lower AI displacement risk or stronger transition opportunities. These pathways are designed to help workers and workforce partners identify occupations that may share similar skills, offer comparable wages, require attainable education or training, or provide stronger long-term labor market prospects.

1. Compare Risk Explore occupations with higher or lower AI displacement risk.
2. Review Labor Market Data Compare employment, wages, openings, and projected growth.
3. Identify Pathways Find related lower-risk occupations and training options.
4. Support Decisions Use results to guide career navigation, planning, and investment.

The goal is to support practical decision-making. A worker may use the tool to identify possible next-step occupations. A workforce board may use it to prioritize training investments. A policymaker may use it to understand which occupations or worker groups may need additional support as AI adoption increases.

How to Interpret the Results

The results should be interpreted as a planning resource, not a definitive forecast. A high AI Displacement Index score does not mean that an occupation will disappear. It means that the occupation has characteristics that may make it more vulnerable to AI-related disruption, especially when theoretical exposure overlaps with labor market weakness or limited transition options.

Similarly, a low score does not mean that an occupation will be unaffected by AI. AI may still change job duties, required skills, tools, workflows, or productivity expectations in lower-risk occupations. The index is intended to identify relative risk across occupations, not to provide certainty about any individual job.

Users should also interpret transition pathways carefully. A related occupation may be a strong comparison because it has similar skills, lower AI displacement risk, better projected demand, or comparable wages. However, individual career decisions should also consider location, prior experience, credentials, licensing requirements, transportation, childcare, training availability, and personal interests.

The best use of this website is to support informed conversations. Workers, career counselors, training providers, employers, and policymakers can use the results to ask better questions about where AI-related changes may occur, what skills may become more important, and which pathways may offer greater resilience.
